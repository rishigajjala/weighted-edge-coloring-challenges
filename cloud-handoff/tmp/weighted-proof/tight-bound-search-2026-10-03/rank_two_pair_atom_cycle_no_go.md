# A scoped rank-two cycle-frustration barrier

All edge weights lie in `(1/3,1]`. A heavy edge has weight `>1/2`; a small edge has weight `<=1/2`. Consider a simple even physical cycle, partitioned into consecutive two-edge atoms at alternating vertices. Each atom consists of one heavy edge of weight `h_i` and one small edge of weight `s_i`, with `h_i+s_i<=1`, so the atom is locally feasible at its center.

At the other alternating vertices, consecutive atoms meet through their outer edges. They **cannot all reject each other**. Indeed, there are as many meetings as atoms and exactly one outer heavy incidence per atom. Since two small edges always fit, rejection at every meeting would require a heavy incidence at every meeting. Hence every meeting would contain exactly one heavy and one small incidence. Orient the meeting from the atom contributing its heavy edge to the atom contributing its small edge. Every atom contributes one heavy and one small outer incidence, so these arcs form a directed cycle. At an arc `i -> j`, rejection says `h_i+s_j>1`, while internal compatibility says `h_i+s_i<=1`; therefore `s_j>s_i`. Strict increase around a directed cycle is impossible.

This rules out the simplest attempt to encode a frustrated odd cycle by compatible heavy-small path atoms in a bipartite graph. It is only a statement about this atomization of a simple physical cycle, not a rank-two hull theorem. Atoms can share vertices outside a simple cycle, have multiple copies, or produce conflicts from four incidences at a vertex. Also, the resulting one-copy cycle has a trivial two-coloring by alternating matchings, so the lemma's direct coloring corollary does not improve a bound.

## Weak local exchange

At any vertex, the graph whose vertices are incident edge occurrences and whose edges are compatible two-item bins has no induced two-edge matching (`2K2`). Two small items always fit; two heavies never do; heavy-small neighborhoods are nested. In particular, any two disjoint compatible bins of size two have at least one compatible cross pair. The only nontrivial case has two heavy-small bins `(h_1,s_1)` and `(h_2,s_2)`; if both cross pairs failed, adding `h_1+s_2>1` and `h_2+s_1>1` would contradict the two original fit inequalities. This gives a one-pair-retaining uncrossing at a repeated vertex of an alternating trail. It does not retain both pairs, and repeated use might lose half the pairings, so it alone does not reach the one-third loss budget for a `4/3` rounding.

For `k` disjoint feasible two-item bins, orient each as anchor `a_i` plus a small partner `s_i`, and sort the partners increasingly. The cyclic reassignment `a_i` to `s_{i-1}` for `i=2,...,k`, and `a_1` to `s_k`, retains the first `k−1` pairs by monotonicity; only the wrap pair may fail. This is a one-loss exchange for any number of bins, but the resulting permutation need not split the global auxiliary components in the desired way.

The one-loss possibility is sharp when trying to align prescribed pairs. Choose `1/3<s_1<...<s_k<1/2` with equal step `ε>0`, put `h_1=1−s_k`, and put `h_i=1−s_i+ε/2` for `i≥2`. All `h_i` are heavy. The cyclic shifted pairs `(h_1,s_k)` and `(h_i,s_{i-1})` fit, but `k−1` designated diagonal pairs `(h_i,s_i)` for `i≥2` fail. Hence local pairability alone gives no one-third guarantee for a fixed physical partner alignment.
